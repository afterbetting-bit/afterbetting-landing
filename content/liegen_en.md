# Gambling addict lying: why it happens and how to help

I gambled for 8 years. During those years I lied a lot. Not because I wanted to be a liar, but because the gambling needed it.

I said everything was fine. That it was just a distraction. When I could not come along to something, I had a reason. The real reason was that I had no money, because it had all gone on gambling.

This article is for two kinds of readers. The partner, parent or friend who wonders why someone they love keeps lying. And the person gambling, who notices the lies have started to run their life. I was the second one.

## Why does a gambling addict lie?

From the outside, lying looks like a choice. From the inside it does not feel like one. For me it happened on its own.

A few things are going on at once:

- **Shame.** You are ashamed of what you have lost. Telling the truth means saying that shame out loud.
- **The hope of winning it back.** As long as you believe you can make up the loss, the lie feels temporary. You will win it back soon, and then nobody needs to know. Why that never works is in [chasing losses in gambling](https://afterbetting.com/blog/chasing-losses-gambling).
- **Protecting other people.** You do not want your partner or parents to worry. So you keep it from them. It feels like protecting them. Mostly, it protects the gambling.
- **Keeping room to gamble.** Once someone knows, gambling gets harder. The lie keeps the door open.

None of these make it right. But they explain why someone who wants to be honest keeps lying anyway.

## How small lies get bigger

It starts small. A number you round down. An evening you describe differently. You say you cannot come because you are tired.

Every lie needs another one. If you say your money is fine, you have to explain why you cannot join in. If you say you have stopped, you have to hide where you were that night. They stack up.

At some point the lies start running your life. You keep track of what you told whom. You plan conversations ahead. You spend more energy hiding than living, every single day.

## The lies you often hear

If you live with someone who gambles, you may recognise a pattern. A few things many gamblers say. I said the first three a lot myself:

- **"Everything is fine."** Even when nothing is.
- **"It is just a distraction."** Making the gambling small, so it does not look like a problem.
- **"I cannot, I am busy."** When the real reason is money.
- **"The money went on other things."** An explanation for a gap that should not be there.
- **"I have stopped."** Sometimes meant in the moment, and no longer true a few days later.

It is rarely one big lie. It is a habit of small ones.

## Lying to yourself

The first person a gambling addict lies to is usually themselves. I knew something was wrong long before I was willing to admit it. Still, I kept telling myself it was not that bad. That I had it under control. That I would stop after the next win.

As long as you lie to yourself, lying to others is easy. You only tell them what you are already telling yourself.

Not sure whether you have a problem? Take the [gambling addiction test](https://afterbetting.com/blog/gambling-addiction-test). Nine questions, two minutes, and nobody sees your answers.

## How to help when someone you love is lying

Being lied to by someone you love hurts. You start doubting yourself. You start checking. You wonder what else is not true.

A few things that can help:

- **It is not about you.** The lies come from the gambling and the shame, not from how little they care about you.
- **Do not turn it into an interrogation.** Putting evidence on the table and demanding a confession rarely works. Someone who feels cornered often lies more.
- **Say what you see and how it affects you.** "I can see the money does not add up and I am worried" works better than "You are lying."
- **Protect your own money.** You are allowed to separate your accounts, even if it feels like distrust. It is self-protection.
- **Look after yourself.** You do not have to carry this alone. Helplines are there for family and friends too. In the UK, call the National Gambling Helpline on 0808 8020 133. In the US, call or text 1-800-MY-RESET.

More on the conversation and on looking after yourself in [how to help your partner with a gambling addiction](https://afterbetting.com/blog/my-partner-has-a-gambling-problem).

## If you are the one lying

For me, it never came out. Nobody caught me. I came clean myself. It was hard. It was also the moment the lies could stop.

If you take the step yourself, you decide how the conversation starts. That is different from someone finding out.

- **Tell one person.** You do not have to tell everyone at once.
- **Tell the whole story, including the number.** A second surprise a few weeks later often hurts more than the first.
- **Do not expect it to be fine straight away.** They need time. Anger and sadness are part of it.
- **Show you mean it.** With actions, not promises. Switch on blocks, make your money visible, get help.

How to have that conversation is in [how to tell your family about a gambling problem](https://afterbetting.com/blog/how-to-tell-family-about-gambling-problem). ## Frequently asked questions

**Does every gambling addict lie?**
Not everyone who gambles lies. But a gambling problem almost always comes with hiding: money, time, how bad it really is. Lying is often one of the first signs that gambling has become a problem.

**How do I know if my partner is lying about gambling?**
Often through money that does not add up, unexplained withdrawals, secrecy around their phone, and excuses not to join in. More signs in [signs of a gambling addiction](https://afterbetting.com/blog/gambling-addiction-symptoms-and-signs).

**Does the lying stop when the gambling stops?**
Usually, but not always straight away. Lying has become a habit. Clear agreements about openness, for example about money, help.

**Can trust really come back?**
Yes, but it takes time. It comes back through behaviour that checks out, not through promises.
