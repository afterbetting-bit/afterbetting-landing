Gambling withdrawal symptoms: what to expect when you stop

Emptiness, triggers and lying awake. What I noticed in the first weeks, and what helped.

By the founder of Afterbetting · 17 October 2026 · 8 min read

The first weeks after I stopped were not calm.

I thought the hardest part would be making the decision. It was not. The hardest part came after.

Emptiness. Triggers when I least expected them. And waking up in the middle of the night with one thought: gambling.

That is what a lot of people call gambling withdrawal. In this piece I share what I noticed, what others often describe, how long it lasted for me and what helped.

One thing first: I am not a doctor. This is not a medical list. It is my experience, plus what I hear from many others in recovery. If you are worried about what you are feeling, or things are getting worse, talk to your GP. At the bottom you will find where else to go.

What I noticed in the first weeks

The emptiness

Gambling took up space. Not just time, but my head too. Planning, waiting for results, doing the sums, working out how I would win it back.

When I stopped, that space was suddenly empty. And empty does not feel like peace. It feels like something is missing.

Especially evenings and weekends. Those were my gambling hours. Work gave my days a shape. But in the evenings there was nothing left to fill that slot.

Triggers I did not see coming

An advert. A match on TV. A notification. A Friday night.

Some triggers I knew about. Others caught me off guard. One minute there was nothing, the next I was thinking about a bet.

Waking up at night

This was maybe the hardest part. Waking up in the middle of the night and the thought is just there. Phone next to the bed. Nobody watching.

Those are the moments you feel most alone with the urge.

What others often describe

What I went through is not unusual. When I talk to other people in recovery, or read what they write, the same things keep coming up:

  • Restlessness. Not being able to sit still, wanting to do something without knowing what.
  • Emptiness or boredom. Especially at the times you used to gamble.
  • Poor sleep. Trouble falling asleep, or waking up in the night.
  • Irritability. Shorter fuse, easily annoyed.
  • Constant thoughts about gambling. Thoughts that keep coming back, even when you do not want them.
  • Feeling low or anxious. Especially when money worries and debt are weighing on you too.

Not everyone gets all of these. Some people notice very little, others a lot. It says nothing about how strong you are.

How long does it last?

For me it got better after a few weeks. Not all at once, but step by step. The emptiness shrank. The triggers came less often. And above all, my sleep came back. Better than it had been in years.

That is my experience, not a rule. For some people it goes quicker, for others it takes longer. What I can say is this: it does not stay the way it is in those first weeks.

Why the first weeks feel like this

For me, gambling was more than placing bets. It was excitement. It was an escape from everything else. It was something to look forward to on an empty evening.

When you stop, all of that goes at once. And nothing replaces it straight away.

At the same time, everything else comes into view. While you are gambling, you can push the debt, the lies and the tension at home slightly out of sight. Once you stop, it is all right in front of you. That makes the first weeks heavy, even apart from the urges.

That is not a sign stopping is not working. It is a sign you can finally see where you stand. You can build from there.

A plan for the first weeks

Looking back, the most important thing is this: do not wait for the hard moments to arrive. Fill them in advance.

  • Plan your evenings and weekends. Write down what you will do at your old gambling times. The more specific, the better.
  • Block everything you can. Self-exclude through GamStop and put blocking software on your phone and laptop. In a bad moment you want as few choices as possible.
  • Tell one person. Someone who knows you have stopped and who you can call or text when it is hard, even late at night.
  • Make a list for the nights. What will you do if you wake up wanting to gamble? Drink some water, read, write down how you feel. If you decide beforehand, you do not have to work it out at 3am.
  • Schedule your exercise. Put fixed times in your week to train or walk, like an appointment.
  • Keep track of how it goes. A few words a day is enough. After a few weeks you will see it getting better, even if it does not feel that way on the day.

What helped me

Routine

This was the foundation for me. Fixed times to get up and go to bed. Fixed things at fixed times, especially in the evenings and at weekends.

It sounds boring. It is. But routine fills the space gambling used to take. The fewer empty hours, the less room for the urge.

Exercise

Moving helped with the restlessness. After a workout my head was quieter. And I slept better.

It does not have to be a marathon. A walk, a bike ride, an hour at the gym. The point is giving the restlessness somewhere to go.

Thinking more positively

In those weeks my mind went to dark places fast. "This will never work." "I have ruined everything." "Once will not hurt."

I started to push back on purpose. Not pretending everything was fine, but looking at what was going well. Another evening without a bet. Another day closer to where I wanted to be.

Reading about self-development

Reading gave my mind something else to do. Books on self-development helped me look forward instead of back.

For the nights

What helped when I woke up at night: no phone next to the bed, gambling sites and apps blocked, and letting the thought come without acting on it. It passes. More on sleep in sleep after stopping gambling.

And when the urge is strong, day or night: do gambling urges go away.

If it goes wrong during those weeks

Maybe you gamble once in those first weeks anyway. It happens more often than people admit.

It does not mean it was all for nothing. The weeks you did not gamble still count. What you learned about your triggers and your empty hours is not lost.

What matters most is what you do the next day. Not carrying on because "it is ruined anyway". Starting again, and looking at what led up to that evening. More on that in what to do after a gambling relapse.

When to get help

Withdrawal is part of stopping for a lot of people. But you do not have to do it alone. Get help if:

  • you feel low, hopeless or anxious and it is not easing,
  • you are not sleeping or not coping day to day,
  • you notice you are drinking more or using something else to take the edge off,
  • you are having thoughts of suicide.

Your GP is a good first step. In the UK, the National Gambling Helpline (GamCare) is free and open 24/7 on 0808 8020 133, and the NHS runs gambling clinics you can refer yourself to. If you are thinking about suicide, call Samaritans on 116 123, or 999 in an emergency.

For a full overview, read where to get help for gambling addiction.

Finally

The first weeks are hard. It helps to know that before you start, or while you are in it.

But it does not stay that way. For me it got better step by step. The emptiness faded. The nights got quiet. And my sleep became better than it had been in years.

If you are in those first weeks right now: hold on. Not forever. Just for today.

Frequently asked questions

Is gambling withdrawal real?

Many people notice restlessness, emptiness, poor sleep, irritability or constant thoughts about gambling in the first weeks after stopping. Not everyone experiences the same things. If you are worried, talk to your GP.

How long does gambling withdrawal last?

It varies from person to person. For me it got better step by step after a few weeks, and my sleep in particular came back. For some it is quicker, for others it takes longer.

What helps with gambling withdrawal?

What helped me was routine, exercise, consciously thinking more positively and reading about self-development. Blocking gambling sites and keeping my phone away from the bed made the nights easier.

When should I get help?

If you feel low or hopeless and it is not easing, if you are not sleeping or coping, or if you have thoughts of suicide. See your GP, call the National Gambling Helpline on 0808 8020 133, or Samaritans on 116 123.
