Recovery

Who are you without gambling?

Why stopping can leave you feeling empty, and how you start building someone new.

By Afterbetting · updated 11 October 2026 · 8 min read

You stopped gambling. The bets are gone. The apps are gone. And somewhere in the first weeks, a question you did not expect starts to come up.

Who am I now?

Most writing about gambling addiction is about money, relationships and health. Those matter. But this question is one of the reasons stopping feels harder than people think. It is rarely talked about, so when it hits, many people think something is wrong with them.

Nothing is wrong with you. This page is about that question, and how you start answering it.

Gambling was more than a habit

For a lot of people, gambling slowly becomes part of how they see themselves. Not just something they do. Something they are.

The one who knows. Especially with sports betting. You know the teams, the form, the odds. You have a system. People ask you for tips. That knowledge feels like part of who you are.

The social side. The group chat with tips. Watching matches together. The language, the stories about the big win and the near miss.

The way you cope. Gambling as the way you deal with stress, boredom or a flat week. The thing that makes an ordinary evening feel like something.

When you stop, all of that goes at once.

What it was like for me

From the founder: I spent 8 years as a sports bettor. When I stopped, I did not know who I was without it. I had been the person who knew every team, every player, every statistic. Without that, I felt genuinely empty. Not sad about the money. Empty about the person.

That emptiness is what this page is about. It is real. It is also not permanent.

Why it feels so empty at first

When gambling goes, it takes several things with it: something to think about, people to share it with, a way to handle feelings and a story about yourself.

What is left in the first weeks is a gap. Free evenings with nothing in them. A head that keeps going back to old routines. Conversations where you do not know what to say anymore.

That is why early recovery can feel worse than people expect. It is not only about missing gambling. It is about missing a version of yourself.

The gap does not fill itself. You have to fill it, on purpose and a bit at a time.

Four places to start rebuilding

1. What did you care about before gambling took over?

For most people, gambling slowly pushed other things out. Think back. What did you enjoy before? Playing sport rather than betting on it? Music? Cooking? Building or fixing things? Being outside?

Those are not childish things to go back to. They are parts of you that were pushed aside.

2. Let what you do every day become who you are

Identity is not something you think up. It grows out of what you do, again and again. Someone who walks every evening becomes a walker. Someone who writes a few lines every night becomes someone who reflects. Someone who checks their money every week becomes someone who looks after it.

Start small. One habit you do every day, linked to something you already do. Daily habits after gambling has more on how to keep it going.

3. Rebuild the social side

If your social life was built around gambling, you need new places to be with people. A sports club where you play instead of bet. A weekly dinner with someone. Volunteering. A support group.

And tell at least one person what you are going through. It is very hard to build a new life while hiding the old one. How to tell someone you have a gambling problem helps with that conversation.

4. Find a new way to handle hard moments

If gambling was how you dealt with stress or emptiness, you need something to replace that job. Not one thing, but a few you can choose from: a walk, a phone call, writing it down, exercise, waiting 15 minutes until the worst has passed.

The recovery kit in the app is built for those moments.

What to do with the sports fan in you

If you were a sports bettor, you may wonder whether you can still watch football or tennis. There is no rule that works for everyone.

For many people it helps to take a break from watching in the first months, especially alone or with people who bet. Later, some find they can enjoy the sport again for what it is. Others find it keeps pulling them back and choose to let it go. Both are fine. Be honest with yourself about which one you are.

Shame and the old you

Part of the identity question is shame. About what you did, the money, the lies. It is tempting to want to erase the old version of yourself completely.

You do not have to. The person who gambled is also the person who stopped. Both are you. You do not have to be proud of everything to accept that it happened. And the fact that you are reading this, trying to work out who to become, already says something about who you are now.

A year from now

The new identity does not arrive on day one. It does not come with a big moment either. It grows quietly, out of ordinary days.

At some point you will notice that you think about gambling less. That you have things you look forward to that have nothing to do with odds. That people know you for something else.

That is not a lucky break. That is what you built.

Start with one thing this week. One old interest, one habit, one person. That is enough for now.

Frequently asked questions

Why do I feel empty after I stopped gambling?

For many people gambling was more than a habit. It gave them something to think about, people to share it with, a way to handle feelings and a story about themselves. When it stops, all of that goes at once and leaves a gap that needs to be filled on purpose.

How do I find myself again after gambling addiction?

Start small: pick up something you enjoyed before gambling took over, build one daily habit, find new places to be with people and tell at least one person what you are going through. Identity grows out of what you do repeatedly.

Can I still watch sport after quitting sports betting?

There is no rule that fits everyone. Many people take a break from watching in the first months. Later some can enjoy sport again for what it is, while others find it keeps pulling them back and let it go.
