Student gambling addiction: signs, numbers and where to get help

Gambling is common at university. When it stops being a night out and starts running your life.

By Afterbetting · 21 November 2026 · 5 min read

The short answer

Gambling is common at university. In the UK the 2026 Annual Student Gambling Survey found that 65% of students had gambled in the past year, and that students who gamble lose over £50 a week on average. Signs it has become a problem: betting with loan money, missing lectures, borrowing from friends, lying about it. Your university can help, and so can the National Gambling Helpline on 0808 8020 133.

University gives you freedom, a student loan in one go, a phone full of apps and friends who bet on the weekend. For a lot of people that is fine. For some it is how a habit becomes something that runs your life.

I started gambling at 16 and gambled for eight years, mostly on sports. Through my late teens and early twenties it controlled my life, almost 24/7. I shut myself off from people and sank deeper. I ran up more than €40,000 in debt. If some of this sounds familiar, this page is for you.

How common it is

Every year the charity Ygam and the self-exclusion service Gamstop survey 2,000 UK university students. The 2026 report found:

  • 65% of students had gambled at least once in the past year.
  • Students who gamble lose over £50 a week on average, a rise of almost 50% in one year.
  • 46% of students who gamble said it had a negative effect on their university experience.

So if it feels like everyone around you bets, you are not imagining it. That is also what makes it hard to notice when your betting is different.

Why students are at higher risk

  • The loan arrives in one go. A large amount in your account at once makes a big bet feel small.
  • Friends. Betting is part of how a lot of groups watch sport. Saying no can feel like leaving the group.
  • Time. Long gaps between lectures, evenings alone in a room, weekends with nothing fixed.
  • Alcohol. A few drinks and the next bet feels smaller than it is. For me alcohol and betting went together.
  • The phone. Every app is always there. There is no walk to a betting shop to change your mind on.
  • Stress. Exams, money, loneliness. Betting gives you something else to think about, until it becomes the thing you worry about.

Signs it is becoming a problem

  • You bet with money meant for rent, food or books.
  • You bet to win back what you lost.
  • You borrow from friends, family or apps, or take extra credit.
  • You miss lectures or deadlines because of betting, or because of the night after.
  • You lie to friends or parents about money.
  • You feel low, anxious or ashamed, and betting is the only thing that takes the edge off.
  • You pull back from people. You stop going out unless there is a bet on.

That last one was the clearest sign for me. Gambling took more and more space and I shut myself off, which made everything worse. If you see more than one of these, take the anonymous gambling addiction test.

What to do this week

  1. Block it. Sign up to GamStop, install a free blocker like BetBlocker, and turn on the gambling block in your banking app. Step by step in block gambling sites and payments.
  2. Protect your loan. Move rent and essentials to a separate account or pay them in advance. Keep only a weekly amount on the card you use.
  3. Tell one person. A friend, a flatmate, a parent. How to tell your family has words you can use.
  4. Use your university. Student support, money advice and wellbeing teams deal with gambling more often than you think, and it is confidential.
  5. Call the helpline. National Gambling Helpline 0808 8020 133, free, 24/7.

Saying no to betting with friends

The hardest part is often not the apps but the group. Everyone has a bet on, the group chat is full of slips, and you are the one who does not join in.

You do not owe anyone a long explanation. Some lines that work:

  • "I'm off it for a while, I'll just watch."
  • "Money's tight this term."
  • "I bet too much last year, I'm done with it."

Most friends will not care as much as you fear. If one keeps pushing, that tells you something too. Mute the group chat on match days if you need to.

Gambling and your mental health

Betting and feeling low often go together. Sometimes you bet to escape stress or loneliness, and then the losses make it worse. If you notice that you feel anxious, low or ashamed most days, talk to your university wellbeing team or your GP as well. More in gambling, depression and anxiety.

If a friend is betting too much

Maybe this page is not about you but about a flatmate. Talk to them on a quiet day, not after a loss. Say what you have noticed, without lecturing: "You seem stressed about money and you're always on the apps. Are you okay?" Point them to the helpline and to student support. You cannot fix it for them, but being the one who noticed can matter a lot.

If you have debt

Do not try to win it back. That was the most expensive idea I ever had. Write down every debt, talk to your university's money advice team or a free debt charity, and make a plan. I paid mine back in almost two years. It is possible, one month at a time. More in rebuild your finances after gambling.

Filling the time

The hours you used to bet do not disappear. Evenings and weekends were the hardest for me. What helped was a routine and sport. At university that can be a club, a gym slot at a fixed time, a part-time job, anything that puts you around people at the hours you would have been alone with your phone.

You are not the only one

Students who gamble often think they are the only one in their group who has lost control. The numbers say otherwise. Recovery is common too. The sooner you start, the less you have to pay back.

Frequently asked questions

How many students gamble?

In the 2026 Annual Student Gambling Survey by Ygam and Gamstop, 65% of UK university students had gambled in the past year, and students who gamble lost over £50 a week on average.

Can my university help with a gambling problem?

Yes. Student support, wellbeing and money advice teams can help confidentially, and many universities work with gambling support charities.

I gambled my student loan. What now?

Stop and do not try to win it back. Block gambling sites and payments, tell someone, and talk to your university money advice team about rent and essentials. The National Gambling Helpline is on 0808 8020 133.

Is betting with friends a problem?

Not always. It becomes a problem when you bet more than you planned, chase losses, borrow, lie about it or feel you cannot stop.
