How to stop gambling and save money

From losing, to paying off, to building. What I did, and why you're allowed to treat yourself along the way.

By Afterbetting · 31 October 2026 · 7 min read

When I stopped gambling, I owed more than €40,000. Saving wasn't a word that fitted my life back then. There was only a hole, and it had to be filled.

Almost two years later the debt was gone. And then came a question I hadn't expected: now what? Because the money that had gone to gambling for years, and then to paying off the debt, was suddenly just there.

This article is about that step. From losing, to paying off, to building. What I did, what I'd do differently, and why you're allowed to treat yourself along the way.

What gambling was really costing you

The first step is knowing what you're dealing with. Most people who gamble don't know what they were losing each week. That's no accident. Not knowing is easier.

Here's a sum that makes a lot of people go quiet: if 50 a week went to gambling on average, that's 2,600 a year. At 100 a week it's 5,200. That's money you keep from now on, every week you don't gamble. Euros, pounds or dollars, the maths is the same.

Work out your own number with the how much have I lost gambling calculator. It's anonymous and runs in your browser. That number becomes your savings amount.

First: is there still debt?

If you have debt, paying it off comes before saving. Interest on a loan or credit card is almost always higher than what a savings account pays. A small emergency fund is still sensible, so a broken washing machine doesn't push you back into borrowing.

This is how I did it:

  • I wrote down the real number. Every debt, every person, the full total. I'd avoided looking at it for years. Seeing it on paper was awful, but for the first time it was a number instead of a fog.
  • One fixed amount every month. No deciding each month how much I could spare. The deciding was exactly where it always went wrong.
  • Anything extra went to the debt. Everything that came in above that fixed amount. Every extra euro. Not some of it. All of it.

It took almost two years. The hardest part was working hard without seeing anything back straight away. And having nothing to show the people around you. Nobody sees you paying off a debt. You do it for yourself.

From paying off to saving: keep the rhythm

When the debt was gone, I did something simple. The fixed amount that had gone to the debt every month went to savings from then on. Same day, same amount, different account.

That sounds obvious, but it's the moment a lot of people let the money leak away. The debt is gone, suddenly there's room, and that room fills itself with spending. A few months later you can't say where it went.

If you keep the rhythm, you barely notice. You were already living on that amount. The only difference is where the rest goes: not to the past anymore, but to later.

Give yourself something too

There's one thing I do differently from when I was paying off the debt. I now deliberately spend a bit more on myself. Not a lot, but enough to feel that all that work is paying off.

That isn't weakness. For two years everything went into a hole nobody could see. If quitting gambling only ever feels like giving things up, it's harder to keep going. You're allowed to notice your life getting better. A dinner out, something for your home, a weekend away. Planned, paid with money you have, and without guilt.

The difference from before: you choose it in advance. It isn't an impulse after a win or a loss.

How to make saving easy

A few things that help, especially if you used to gamble:

  1. Save on payday, automatically. Set up a standing transfer for the day your salary comes in. What you don't see, you don't miss. And what's already gone can't be bet.
  2. Make it hard to reach. A savings account without a card, or at a different bank, adds friction. On a bad night you don't want your savings two taps away from a betting site.
  3. Start with an emergency fund. First enough for unexpected costs. Then keep going. A buffer takes the panic out of setbacks, and panic used to be a reason to gamble.
  4. Give the money a goal. "Saving" is vague. "Three months of fixed costs" or "a deposit for a home" isn't. A goal makes it easier to leave the money alone.
  5. Keep your gambling blocks on. Even when things are going well. A growing balance can feel like play money on a weak night. So don't switch off your blocks because you're "done with it anyway".

A simple plan for your first month of saving

Start small and concrete. You can do this month:

  1. Week 1: put the number on paper. How much went to gambling each week? Use the calculator. Write down your fixed costs and what you really need to live on.
  2. Week 2: open a separate account. Without a card, or at another bank. Give it a name that reminds you why: "buffer", "home", "free".
  3. Week 3: set up the transfer. On payday, automatically. Start with an amount you can definitely spare. Small and steady beats big and reversed.
  4. Week 4: pick your reward. Something small, planned, paid with money you have. Put it in your calendar. That way the first month doesn't only feel like giving things up.

After that month, look back. Did it work? Raise the amount a little. Didn't it? Look at where it went wrong, without beating yourself up. Saving has setbacks too.

What matters most is that it becomes a habit. Just like not gambling. A day, a week, a month. At some point you look at your savings account and see something you didn't see for years: money that's yours.

Watch out for quick money

If you used to gamble, you know the feeling of quick money. That feeling can show up in other forms: crypto that's "sure to go up", day trading, a tip from a friend. It isn't called gambling, but it can press the same button.

I'm not going to tell you what to do with your money. This isn't financial advice. But be honest with yourself. Does it feel like a buzz, do you want to check it every hour, are you thinking about "winning it back"? Then for you it may not be saving anymore.

Slow and boring is exactly right. That's how the debt disappeared too.

What paying off doesn't fix

One thing nobody told me in advance: paying off a debt doesn't erase it from your history. Here in the Netherlands the registration on my credit file stays for five years after the last loan is closed. I still can't get a mortgage because of it. In the UK, a default usually stays on your credit file for six years.

That's frustrating. But it's no reason to stop saving. If anything, when you do apply for a mortgage, your own savings and a few years of clean bank statements make you stronger. More in will gambling affect my mortgage application?.

What you get back each year

Quitting gambling pays off in a way you can see. Not straight away, but a little more every month.

  • 25 a week: 1,300 a year, 3,900 after three years.
  • 50 a week: 2,600 a year, 7,800 after three years.
  • 100 a week: 5,200 a year, 15,600 after three years.
  • 250 a week: 13,000 a year, 39,000 after three years.

That's without interest, and without everything else gambling cost you: fees, interest on loans, missed chances. The real difference is bigger.

Frequently asked questions

How much money will I save if I stop gambling?

Exactly what you used to lose each week, times 52. At 50 a week that's 2,600 a year. Work out your own number with the calculator.

Should I pay off debt first or save first?

Usually pay off debt first, with a small emergency fund for unexpected costs. Interest on debt is almost always higher than what saving earns. If you're unsure, free debt advice is available, for example from StepChange in the UK.

How do I stop myself gambling my savings?

Save automatically on payday, into an account without a card or at another bank, and keep your gambling blocks on. That way there's always a barrier between a bad night and your savings.

Is it okay to spend money on myself when I've just quit?

Yes, as long as you choose it in advance and it's money you have. Recovery should give you something you can feel. That helps you keep going.

Is investing a good idea after a gambling addiction?

I can't decide that for you, and this isn't financial advice. But be honest: if it feels like a buzz or quick money, it may be too close to gambling for you.
