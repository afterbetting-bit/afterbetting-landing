My dad or mum has a gambling problem: what can I do?

Whether you still live at home or moved out years ago: what you can do, what you can't, and where to get support.

By Afterbetting · 25 October 2026 · 9 min read

You've noticed it for a while. Money that goes missing. Stories that don't add up. A phone that's always face down on the table. And now you're fairly sure: your dad or mum has a gambling problem.

It's a strange place to be. Your parent is supposed to look after you, not the other way round. You might feel ashamed, or angry, or both at once. You might wonder whether you're reading too much into it.

This page is for you, whether you still live at home or moved out years ago. It covers what you can do, what you can't, and where to get support. If it's your brother or sister who gambles, there's a section for you further down.

First, the most important thing: it's not your fault

The person who gambles is responsible for their gambling. That stays true even if your parent says otherwise, or blames stress, money worries or the family. Childline says the same to young people in this situation: you're not responsible for your parent's gambling.

The second thing to know: you can't make your parent stop. That step has to come from them. It sounds harsh, but it also takes something off your shoulders. You don't have to fix this. You can help, and you can look after yourself.

How do you recognise a gambling problem in a parent?

Gambling is hard to spot. There's no smell and no slurred speech, like with alcohol. What you usually see is the fallout. Some signs that come up again and again:

  • Money doesn't add up. Bills go unpaid, there's borrowing from relatives, things disappear or get sold.
  • Secrecy. Bank statements get hidden, the phone never leaves their hand, there are betting or casino apps and notifications.
  • Mood swings. Suddenly cheerful and generous, then irritable or low, often for no clear reason.
  • Lying about small things. Where the money went, where they were, why something hasn't been paid.
  • Lost time. Hours on the phone or laptop, up late at night, plans forgotten.

One sign on its own doesn't say much. A pattern of several, especially around money, says more. You can read more in gambling addiction symptoms and signs, and why lying so often comes with gambling in why gambling addicts lie.

If you still live at home

If you're young and living with your parents, you often feel the effects most. Children of parents who gamble often describe things like:

  • getting less attention and having to do more on your own
  • things that used to be possible suddenly aren't, because there's no money
  • arguments between your parents where you feel you have to pick a side
  • shame, so you'd rather not bring friends home
  • mixed feelings: angry or sad, and at the same time still loving your parent

That last one is normal. You can love your dad or mum and be furious about what's happening.

What you can do:

  • Talk to an adult you trust. That could be the parent who doesn't gamble, a grandparent, aunt or uncle, a teacher or someone at school whose job it is to listen.
  • Keep doing what you enjoy. Sport, friends, your hobbies. Don't let the worry at home take over.
  • Don't take on jobs that aren't yours. You don't have to watch the money, sort the bills or bring up your younger siblings. If that's happening now, tell an adult.
  • Talk to someone in confidence. In the UK you can call Childline free on 0800 1111, or talk to a counsellor online. GamCare also supports families of people who gamble.

Don't feel safe at home? If there's violence, or you're being threatened, even if it's "only" about money, call the National Domestic Abuse Helpline on 0808 2000 247. It's free and open 24 hours a day. If you're in immediate danger, call 999.

If you're an adult and worried about your parent

A lot of people searching "my dad is a gambling addict" or "my mum has a gambling problem" are adults themselves. They watch their parent slide and don't know where they stand. Is it your place to say something? Should you?

The answer: you're allowed to say you're worried. You're an adult too, and this affects you. What you can't do is take the problem over.

With older parents there's sometimes a pattern of its own. After retirement, a divorce or losing a partner, a lot of time and quiet opens up. Online gambling is always within reach, day and night, without anyone seeing. That's not an excuse, but it can help you understand where it comes from.

Starting the conversation

There's no perfect moment. There are better and worse ones. A few things that help:

  • Pick a calm moment. Not straight after a loss, not during a row, not when your parent has been drinking.
  • Start with what you see and feel. "I've noticed money keeps disappearing and I'm worried" lands better than "You're a gambling addict".
  • Expect denial. Almost everyone with a gambling problem denies it at first, or plays it down. That doesn't mean the conversation failed. You've planted a seed.
  • Offer something concrete. For example: "Shall we call the National Gambling Helpline together?" or "Do you want me to come with you to the GP?"
  • Keep it short. Ten minutes that end well are worth more than an evening that falls apart.

Money: be clear, even when it's hard

This is often the most painful part. Your parent asks for money, for rent, for food, or "just until payday". What do you do?

  • Don't lend money. Not even "just this once". With a gambling problem there's a real chance the money, directly or indirectly, ends up being gambled.
  • Pay a bill directly rather than handing over cash, if you genuinely want to help with something like rent or energy. That way you know where it goes.
  • Don't take anything out in your name. No loans, no guarantor agreements, no credit cards, no phone contracts.

Saying no to your own dad or mum feels wrong. But it doesn't only protect you. It also makes sure the gambling isn't being paid for by you.

What about your other parent?

Often there's another parent, who's usually hit hardest. They're the partner of someone with a gambling problem, with questions of their own: about money, trust, staying or leaving. Point them to my partner has a gambling problem. You don't have to be the go-between.

Can you block your parent from gambling?

In the UK, self-exclusion schemes like GamStop only work if the person signs up themselves. You can't register someone else, except in specific cases such as a Power of Attorney. Gambling blocks on bank cards also have to be switched on by the account holder.

What you can do is make it easier for them to take that step, by sitting with them while they sign up or helping set up a block on their phone. Read can you exclude someone else from gambling for what is and isn't possible, in the UK and elsewhere.

Is gambling addiction hereditary?

It's a question many children of gamblers quietly ask themselves. The honest answer: your parent doesn't decide what happens to you, but it's worth being aware.

Dutch youth care guidelines describe that children of parents with an addiction are more likely to develop addiction problems themselves later on, up to twice as likely. That research is about addiction in general, not gambling specifically. It also says a large part of that risk comes from the situation at home, not just from genes.

What does that mean for you? No reason to panic. It is a reason to look honestly at your own habits. Do you gamble, even "just for fun"? Do you find it hard to stop, or bet more than you meant to? Then try the gambling addiction test. Knowing where you stand is always better than not knowing.

If it's your brother or sister

Much of the above applies if your brother or sister gambles. A few things are different:

  • You're on the same level. You don't have authority, but you often have more trust than your parents do. Your sibling might tell you things they'd never say at home.
  • Don't let yourself become the secret-keeper. "Don't tell Mum and Dad" is a heavy ask. You don't have to pass everything on, but you don't have to lie either.
  • Here too: don't lend money. Between siblings it happens easily, and that's exactly where it causes the deepest rifts.

Look after yourself too

Standing next to someone with a gambling problem, it's easy to lose yourself. You think about it at night, you check, you worry about every phone call. A few things to remember:

  • You're allowed boundaries. "I'm not lending you money any more" or "I don't want to talk about this today" are healthy sentences.
  • Talk to someone who gets it. The National Gambling Helpline is also there for family and friends. Call free on 0808 8020 133, 24 hours a day. In the US, you can call 1-800-MY-RESET (1-800-697-3738).
  • Getting support for yourself isn't betrayal. You don't have to wait for your parent to get help before you get some for yourself.

Frequently asked questions

My dad gambles but says he has it under control. What now?

Almost everyone with a gambling problem says that. You don't have to win the argument. Say what you see, say you're worried and tell him where he can get help. After that, the choice is his.

Can I tell other people in the family?

Yes. You don't have to carry this alone. Choose someone who'll handle it calmly rather than going straight into a confrontation.

Should I give my mum money if she can't pay her rent otherwise?

Avoid giving cash. If you really want to help, pay the landlord directly, and only if you can afford it. Be honest that this won't become a habit.

Can I make my parent get help?

In practice, no. An adult decides for themselves whether to seek help. What you can do is make that step as easy as possible.

Will I become a gambling addict too?

Not automatically. Children of parents with an addiction are more likely to run into problems of their own. That's a reason to be honest with yourself about your own gambling.

I'm under 18. Who can I talk to?

An adult you trust, or Childline, free on 0800 1111. In the UK, gambling is legal from 18.
